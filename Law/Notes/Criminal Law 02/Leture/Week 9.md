**Part 1: Self-Defense (Crimes Act 1900 NSW)**

Self-defense is a complete defense in New South Wales, meaning a successful plea results in an acquittal. It was simplified from a complex six-step common law process (derived from _Viro_) to a statutory framework and a two-step test.

**Statutory Authorities: Sections 418–421**

The _Crimes Act 1900_ (NSW) provides the legislative basis for self-defense:

|**Section**|**Legal Provision**|**Key Detail**|
|---|---|---|
|**s 418**|**Elements of Self-Defense**|A person is not criminally liable if they believe their conduct is necessary to: (a) defend themselves or another, (b) prevent unlawful deprivation of liberty, (c) protect property, or (d) prevent criminal trespass.|
|**s 419**|**Onus of Proof**|The prosecution bears the onus of negating self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt once the defense is properly raised.|
|**s 420**|**Limitations (Property)**|Self-defense is **not available** for the intentional or reckless infliction of death to protect property or prevent criminal trespass.|
|**s 421**|**Excessive Self-Defense**|A partial defense to murder. If the accused believed the conduct was necessary but the response was unreasonable, murder is reduced to **manslaughter**.|

**The Two-Step Test (_Katarinski_)**

Following the enactment of the legislation, the court in _Katarinski_ established a simplified two-question process for the jury:

1. **Subjective Element:** Did the accused **actually believe** that the force used was necessary?
    - This considers everything personal to the accused: religion, culture, background, IQ, and even intoxication (which may affect subjective belief).
2. **Objective Element:** Was the response **reasonable in the circumstances** as the accused perceived them?
    - The jury does not ask what an "ordinary person" would have done in a vacuum. Instead, they ask if the response was reasonable given what the accused _thought_ was happening.

**Part 2: Duress (Common Law)**

Duress is a common law defense, not found in the _Crimes Act_. It arises when an accused commits a crime because their will was overborne by threats of death or serious injury.

**The Test for Duress (_Abusifah_ and _Lawrence_)**

To successfully raise duress, the following elements must be analyzed:

1. **Subjective Belief:** Did the accused perform the acts because of a threat that death or really serious physical harm (GBH) would be inflicted upon them or their family?
2. **Objective Ordinary Firmness:** Would a person of "ordinary firmness of mind"—possessing the same **age, sex, and maturity** as the accused—have yielded to the threat in the same way?
    - _Note:_ Other personal characteristics like nervousness or anxiety are generally excluded from this objective limb.
3. **No Opportunity for Escape:** The threat must be "present and continuing." If there was a reasonable opportunity to escape or inform the authorities to render the threat ineffective, the defense fails (_Lawrence_).

**Limitations of Duress**

- **Murder:** Duress is generally not a defense to murder. One cannot value their own life (or a family member's) over the life of an innocent victim (_Lynch v Howe_).
- **Voluntary Gang Membership:** Under _Kwame_, if an accused voluntarily joins a criminal group (e.g., a drug cartel or outlaw motorcycle gang) knowing they might be subjected to threats or violence, they cannot later claim duress.
- **Attempted/Constructive Murder:** In NSW, unlike England, duress may arguably apply to attempted murder or constructive murder, subject to limitations regarding criminal groups (_Kwame_).

**Part 3: Key Case Summaries**

**Self-Defense Cases**

**Katarinski**

- **Facts:** A key post-legislation case involving the application of the two-step test.
- **Legal Principles:** Confirmed the move from the old six-step _Viro_ test to a two-step statutory test. It emphasized that s 418(2) is concerned with the reasonableness of the conduct having regard to the accused's state of mind.
- **Exam Relevance:** Primary authority for the two-step test (Subjective belief vs. Objective reasonableness of response based on perceived circumstances).

**Zesivik v DPP**

- **Facts:** A long-running dispute between two neighbors. Zesivik armed himself with a shotgun and killed the neighbor during a confrontation.
- **Legal Principles:** Simplified self-defense to a full defense where the accused honestly believes the action is necessary and there are reasonable grounds for that belief.
- **Exam Relevance:** Historic transition case that moved away from the complexity of _Viro_.

**Viro**

- **Facts:** A 1978 High Court case.
- **Legal Principles:** Established a highly complicated six-step process for self-defense.
- **Exam Relevance:** Useful for demonstrating the "old law" and why the current legislation was needed to simplify the process for juries.

**Conlon**

- **Facts:** The accused was a daily marijuana user who discovered two intruders stealing his crop. He killed both—one by shooting and one by beating with the butt of the gun. He was highly intoxicated.
- **Legal Principles:** Under common law, intoxication could be considered for both what the accused believed and the reasonableness of the response.
- **Exam Relevance:** Crucial for the distinction that **intoxication** can affect the **subjective belief** (Step 1) but cannot make an otherwise unreasonable response **reasonable** (Step 2) in NSW today.

**Kurtig**

- **Facts:** A neighbor dispute where the appellant hit the victim with a tomahawk, causing a depressed skull fracture. Self-defense was the only issue.
- **Legal Principles:** The Crown must eliminate the "reasonable possibility" that the appellant acted in self-defense.
- **Exam Relevance:** Highlights the **onus of proof** (s 419) and the requirement for a judge in a judge-alone trial to direct themselves as if there were a jury.

**Silver**

- **Facts:** Mrs. Silver killed her husband, a violent man on ice who was under police surveillance. Intercepted calls and texts proved he was threatening to kill her and her family. She stabbed him while he was beating her brother.
- **Legal Principles:** The Court of Appeal entered an acquittal, finding the jury's manslaughter verdict unreasonable given the overwhelming evidence of a need for self-defense.
- **Exam Relevance:** Strong example of a "complete defense" under s 418 in a domestic violence context.

**Lisa Mulby Hill (R v Mulby Hill)**

- **Facts:** A former professional footballer had an affair; when the woman moved to Sydney to escape him, he tracked her down and confronted her. He claimed she pulled a knife and "fell on it" during a struggle.
- **Legal Principles:** The accused claimed the death was accidental or self-defense. However, self-defense requires an intentional act because you believe it is necessary, not an "accidental" stabbing.
- **Exam Relevance:** Distinguishes between **accidental killing** and **intentional self-defense**. If the evidence suggests an accident, s 418/s 421 may not apply.

**Trevana**

- **Facts:** A woman in a domestic violence relationship killed her partner (a drug dealer) with a gun. She then attempted to mask the murder, but blood splatter and phone tracking proved her presence.
- **Legal Principles:** First appellate case interpreting s 421. It confirmed that if the accused genuinely believes force is necessary but the force is objectively unreasonable, murder becomes manslaughter.
- **Exam Relevance:** Key authority for **Excessive Self-Defense (s 421)**.

**Duress Cases**

**Lawrence**

- **Facts:** A navigator on ships transporting cannabis from the Philippines. He claimed he only stayed involved because of death threats to him and his family. The ship stopped in Port Moresby, PNG.
- **Legal Principles:** The defense of duress failed because he had a **reasonable opportunity to escape** or inform authorities in Port Moresby but chose to stay.
- **Exam Relevance:** Primary authority for the "no opportunity to escape" requirement.

**Abusifah**

- **Facts:** Accused of armed robbery. Claimed his partner turned the gun on him during the robbery, forcing him to continue.
- **Legal Principles:** Refined the elements of duress: (1) Subjective belief of death/GBH threat, (2) Objective test based on a person of ordinary firmness of mind of the same **age, sex, and maturity**.
- **Exam Relevance:** The "go-to" case for the modern elements of duress.

**Makrinikos**

- **Facts:** A defendant working in a government drug lab allowed bikers to rob the lab of cocaine in exchange for $10,000. He claimed duress and cried in the witness box for three weeks.
- **Legal Principles:** The court rejected the argument that "ordinary firmness of mind" should include the accused's personal characteristics like nervousness or anxiety. Only age, sex, and maturity matter.
- **Exam Relevance:** Strict application of the **objective limb**; limits the personal characteristics a jury can consider.

**Ranjanovic**

- **Facts:** Involved a domestic violence relationship where the accused was forced to assault a man for money.
- **Legal Principles:** Allowed psychiatric evidence regarding **Battered Woman Syndrome** (a type of PTSD) to be considered in determining if a person of ordinary firmness would have yielded to the threats.
- **Exam Relevance:** Exception where expert evidence can assist the jury in understanding why a victim of long-term violence did not "escape."

**Lee**

- **Facts:** Lee strangled his mother after she discovered he was practicing martial arts instead of the piano/studying. He sought to adduce psychiatric evidence about Chinese cultural pressures and his mother's abuse.
- **Legal Principles:** The court refused the expert evidence, stating that the relationship's complexity and cultural pressures were within the "common human experience" of the jury and did not require a psychiatrist.
- **Exam Relevance:** Limits the use of **expert psychiatric evidence** for "ordinary" human experiences or cultural pressures.

**Kwame**

- **Facts:** Involved the "Brothers for Life" gang and charges of murder and attempted murder.
- **Legal Principles:** (1) Voluntary gang membership precludes duress. (2) In NSW, duress **is** available for attempted murder and for accomplices to murder (unlike in England).
- **Exam Relevance:** High-level case for **limitations and gang membership**. Essential for questions involving accomplices.

**Nguyen**

- **Facts:** A gardener hired to grow "tomatoes" in Dubbo discovered he was actually growing marijuana. When he tried to leave, the "mafia" threatened he would never see Sydney again.
- **Legal Principles:** The trial judge initially disallowed the defense, but it was held that the issue of whether the accused voluntarily put themselves in that position should be determined by the jury.
- **Exam Relevance:** Deals with the **voluntary assumption of risk** in criminal enterprises.
