## **1. Conspiracy & The Defense of Impossibility**

### **📌 The Core Rule**

- **Impossibility is NOT a defense** to a conspiracy charge in New South Wales [1, 5].
- **Why?** The offense of conspiracy is complete the moment the **agreement is made** [5]. It focuses purely on the agreement to commit a crime (which is itself intrinsically criminal), regardless of the actual physical outcome [5].
- **NSW Law Reform Commission (Report 129):** Formally recommended that conspiracy can be committed even if facts/circumstances exist that make commission of the offence impossible [6].

### **⚖️ Key Case: _R v El Azzi_ [2004] NSWCCA 455**

- **Facts:** The appellant agreed to manufacture methylamphetamine [1]. However, unknown to the parties, the chemical ingredients supplied (phenylacetic acid) were defective/incapable of actually producing the drug [1, 4].
- **Defense Argument:** The conspiracy was "impossible" to carry out, so no crime occurred [1].
- **Court Decision:** **Appeal dismissed** [5]. The court held that the conspiracy charge was fully valid [5]. The impossibility of completing the manufacture did not wipe out the criminal agreement they had already made [5].
- **Contrast with English Common Law:** Historically, _DPP v Nock_ [1978] AC 979 allowed an impossibility defense [1, 2]. However, NSW courts (following the logic of _R v Shivpuri_ [1987] AC 1 in attempts) have rejected this, making impossibility irrelevant to conspiracy culpability [3, 5, 11].

## **2. The Fault Elements of Conspiracy (Mens Rea)**

### **📌 The General Rule**

At common law, a conspiracy requires a **consensus ad idem** (meeting of minds) to carry out an unlawful act [6].

According to McHugh J in **_Peters v R_ (1998)**, a conspiracy requires [6, 7]:

1. **Intention to Agree:** Both parties must intend to enter into the agreement [6].
2. **Intention to Carry It Out:** At the time of making the agreement, **both parties** must actually intend to carry out the unlawful act [6, 7].
3. **No Conspiracy by Recklessness:** Realizing that the _probable consequence_ of your conduct might be the performance of the unlawful act is **not enough** [7]. There must be specific, actual intent to achieve the unlawful act [7].

> ⚠️ **Acquittal Rule Change (_R v Darby_):** Historically, if one conspirator was acquitted, the other had to be acquitted (as "one cannot conspire with oneself") [7]. Now, one person can be convicted of conspiracy even if the other is acquitted, unless there is a fatal inconsistency in the evidence of the trial [7].

## **3. Conspiracy & Crimes of "Recklessness" (The Money Laundering Pivot)**

### **📌 The Legal Puzzle**

- Can you be guilty of "conspiracy to commit an offence" if the underlying substantive offense only requires **recklessness** as to a circumstance? [8, 9]
- _Example:_ If the substantive crime is "dealing with money being _reckless_ as to whether it is proceeds of crime" [9].

### **⚖️ Key Case: _R v LK; R v RK_ (2010) 241 CLR 177 (High Court of Australia)**

- **Facts:** The respondents were charged with conspiracy to deal with money (>$1M) being reckless that the money was proceeds of crime [9, 11].
- **Crown Argument:** Since the substantive crime only requires "recklessness" about the money being proceeds of crime, a conspiracy to commit that crime should only require recklessness [12, 16].
- **High Court Ruling:** **Crown appeal dismissed** [12].
- **The Rule:** You **cannot** conspire to commit a crime of recklessness without actual **knowledge or belief** of the facts that make the act unlawful [11, 15].
- **Why?** A conspiracy requires an _intention_ to commit the target offence [12, 16]. You cannot "intend" to deal with proceeds of crime unless you actually know or believe that the funds _are_ proceeds of crime at the time of the agreement [11, 15]. Mere recklessness (being aware of a risk) is inconsistent with the specific intent required to form a conspiratorial agreement [12].

## **4. Introduction to Complicity**

### **📌 The "Essence" Distinctions**

- **Attempt:** Trying but failing to commit a crime [17].
- **Conspiracy:** Agreeing to commit a crime [17].
- **Complicity:** Assisting or encouraging others to commit a crime [17].

### **👥 The 4 Categories of Complicity Parties**

When a crime is physically committed by someone, the law categorizes liability as [17]:

1. **The Principal Offender:** The person who physically commits the act [17].
2. **Accessory Before the Fact:** Assists or encourages the crime beforehand (but is _not_ present at the scene) [17].
3. **Aider and Abettor (Principal in the Second Degree):** Present at the scene, assisting and abetting the commission of the crime [17].
4. **Accessory After the Fact:** Assists the offender _after_ the crime has been committed (e.g., helping them escape) [17].
