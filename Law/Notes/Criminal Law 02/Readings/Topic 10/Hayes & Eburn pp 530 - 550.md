Part 1: Self-Defence & Related Defences

The Core Rule: A person is not criminally responsible for an offense if the conduct was carried out in self-defence (Crimes Act NSW, s 418).

The Common Law Test (Zecevic v DPP): To successfully claim self-defence, two questions must be answered:

Subjective: Did the accused actually believe their action was necessary to defend themselves?

Objective: Were there reasonable grounds for that belief?

The Role of Intoxication (R v Katarzynski): Self-induced intoxication can be taken into account when figuring out what the accused actually believed (Subjective). However, it cannot be taken into account when deciding if their response was reasonable (Objective).

Excessive Force resulting in Death (s 421): If an accused uses lethal force believing it was necessary, but a jury decides the level of force was not a reasonable response to the threat, the accused is guilty of manslaughter, not murder.

Part 2: Attempt, Conspiracy, and Complicity (Inchoate Crimes)

Inchoate Crimes: This area of law punishes people for criminal intentions and preparations, even if the actual crime was never fully completed. It includes terrorism preparations, attempts, conspiracy (making an agreement to commit a crime), and complicity (helping someone else commit a crime).

Attempt (s 344A): Attempting to commit a crime demonstrates the same criminal intention as actually doing it. By law, anyone who attempts a crime is liable to the same penalties as if they had successfully committed the offense.

Factual Impossibility (Britten v Alpogut): You can still be convicted of attempting a crime even if completing it was actually impossible. For example, if you intend to smuggle illegal drugs and do everything in your power to do so, you are guilty of an attempt even if the substance turns out to be harmless.
