**1. The Reception of English Law**

- **Terra Nullius:** The 18th-century legal fiction that Australia was "practically unoccupied, without settled inhabitants or settled law". This justified the peaceful annexation and automatic application of English law.
- **Mabo v Queensland (No 2) :** The High Court famously rejected the _terra nullius_ doctrine and recognized **Native Title**.
- **Act of State Doctrine:** The High Court confirmed that the Crown’s initial acquisition of sovereignty is a sovereign act and its validity cannot be challenged in municipal courts.
- **Date of Reception (25 July 1828):** Formalised by the _Australian Courts Act 1828_ (Imperial). All English common law and statutes in force on this date applied to the colony to the extent they were **"suitable to local conditions"**.

**⚖️ Landmark Cases on Reception:**

- **_SGIC v Trigwell_ (1979):** Ms. Rook collided with sheep on a highway. The High Court held that the English rule from _Searle v Wallbank_ (1947)—which exempted landowners from a duty of care to prevent livestock from straying onto roads—was received as part of the common law in 1836 because it was suitable to local grazing conditions at the time. The High Court deferred to the parliament to change the law if they wished.
- **_Cooper v Stewart_ (1889):** In 1823, Governor Brisbane granted land with a clause allowing the Crown to resume up to 10 acres for public use. Sixty years later, the Crown resumed 10 acres for a public park. The owner argued the clause violated the rule against perpetuities. The Privy Council held that the rule against perpetuities was developed for a mature English system and was **unsuitable for a developing "infant colony"** like NSW in 1823.

**2. Autocracy to Responsible Self-Government**

- **Autocratic Era:** Early NSW governors held absolute executive, legislative, and judicial power. The military (NSW Corps) frequently clashed with governors, leading to the **Rum Rebellion of 1808** where Governor William Bligh was deposed.
- **Transition to Self-Government:** Transition occurred as the free settler population grew and demanded representation. Bicameral (two-house) parliaments were established by colonial constitutions (NSW, TAS, VIC in 1855; SA in 1856; QLD in 1860; WA in 1890).
- **Democratisation:** Voting was originally restricted to wealthy male property owners. Adult male suffrage was introduced in the late 19th century, and women's suffrage began with South Australia in 1894.

**3. Imperial Controls & The Path to Independence**

- **Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 (Imperial):** Established that colonial parliaments had broad power to make laws, but their laws were void if they directly conflicted with British laws explicitly extending to the colony (**The Repugnancy Doctrine**).
    - **_Union Steamship Co of NZ v Commonwealth_ (1925):** The High Court struck down sections of the Commonwealth’s _Navigation Act 1912_ because they conflicted with the UK’s _Merchant Shipping Act 1894_ under s 2 of the CLVA.
- **Statute of Westminster 1931 (UK):** A pivotal milestone recognizing dominion equality. It removed the repugnancy doctrine (s 2), confirmed extraterritorial law-making power (s 3), and stopped the UK from legislating for Australia without consent (s 4). **Australia adopted this in 1942**, retroactive to the outbreak of WWII on 3 September 1939.
- **Australia Acts 1986:** Severed the final legal, legislative, and judicial ties to the UK, notably **ending all appeals to the Privy Council** in London.

**4. State Parliaments & State Constitutions**

- **Uncontrolled Constitutions:** Unlike the Commonwealth Constitution, state constitutions are generally "uncontrolled," meaning they are highly flexible and can be amended by ordinary legislation or even by implication.
    - **_McCauley v The King_ (1920):** Confirmed that unless a constitutional clause is entrenched, an ordinary inconsistent Act of parliament implicitly amends the state constitution.
- **Manner and Form Exception:** Parliaments can pass procedural restrictions (e.g., requiring a supermajority or a referendum) to "entrench" and safeguard certain constitutional structures.
    - **_AG (NSW) v Trethowan_ (1931):** The NSW Government tried to abolish the Legislative Council without a referendum, bypassing **Section 7A** of the NSW Constitution. The High Court and Privy Council held Section 7A was a valid, binding manner and form restriction.

**🛠️ Scope of State Legislative Power:**

- **Plenary Power:** s 5 of the _Constitution Act 1902_ (NSW) gives the parliament very broad authority to make laws for the **"peace, welfare, and good government of New South Wales"**.
    - **_Union Steamship v King_ (1988):** Held that "peace, welfare, and good government" does not impose any substantive limit on power; courts cannot strike down laws merely because they are unwise.
- **Extraterritorial Power:** Confirmed by s 2(1) of the _Australia Act 1986_, requiring only a "sufficient connection" to the state (_Mobil Oil v Victoria_ 2002).

**5. Federal Limits & The Kable Doctrine**

- **Constitutional Constraints:** State legislative power is broad but subject to the Commonwealth Constitution:
    - Exclusive Commonwealth powers (e.g., s 52(1), s 90 customs/excise, s 115 currency).
    - The **Implied Freedom of Political Communication** (_Lange v ABC_).
- **_Kable v DPP_ (1996) ("The Kable Doctrine"):**
    - **Facts:** The NSW Parliament passed the _Community Protection Act 1994_ specifically targeting a single individual (Gregory Wayne Kable), directing the Supreme Court to order his preventive detention under a civil standard of proof based on predicted future violence.
    - **Holding:** The High Court struck down the Act.
    - **Legal Principle:** Although state constitutions do not strictly require a separation of powers (_Gilbertson v SA_), state Supreme Courts are vested with federal jurisdiction under s 77 of the Commonwealth Constitution (making them Chapter 3 courts). Therefore, a state parliament **cannot pass laws that compromise the institutional integrity or public confidence in the impartiality** of a state court.
