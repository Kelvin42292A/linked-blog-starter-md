## Material Facts

Abusafiah committed an armed robbery, assaulting a victim and threatening him with a knife to steal his coat. Abusafiah admitted to the act but claimed he was acting under duress. He testified that a violent associate, El Atar, had forced him to do it by pointing a revolver at him and threatening to shoot him.

## Issues

What is the correct objective test for duress, and specifically, should the jury ask whether a person of "ordinary firmness" _would_ have yielded to the threat, or merely if they _might_ have yielded?

## Rule/Law

- The Crown bears the onus of proof; it must eliminate any reasonable possibility that the accused acted under duress.
- The objective test is: Is there a reasonable possibility that a **"person of ordinary firmness of mind and will, and of the same sex and maturity as the accused, would have yielded to that threat"**.
- The court explicitly rejected the idea that the test should be whether an ordinary person _might_ or _could_ have yielded.

## Application/Analysis

The trial judge's directions were slightly flawed (using the term "average person" instead of "person of ordinary firmness"), but it did not cause a miscarriage of justice. The jury clearly did not believe Abusafiah's story that El Atar had actually pulled a gun on him in the first place, so the exact wording of the objective test would not have changed their verdict.

## Conclusion

The NSW Court of Criminal Appeal dismissed the appeal and upheld Abusafiah's conviction,.

### Judges Rules (**Ordinary firmness)**

When assessing the objective element of the duress defence, the law applies the **"Ordinary Firmness"** test:

1. **The "Ordinary Firmness" Standard:** The Crown must prove that there is no reasonable possibility that a **person of ordinary firmness of mind and will** would have yielded to the threat in the way the accused did.
2. **Relevant Characteristics:** This hypothetical person of ordinary firmness is not entirely faceless; they are legally assumed to have the **same sex and maturity (age)** as the accused.
3. **"Would" vs. "Might":** The legal standard is strict. The question is whether the ordinary person **would** have yielded to the threat, not whether they _might_ or _could_ have yielded.
4. **The Burden of Proof:** The accused never has to "prove" they were under duress. Once the issue is raised, the Crown must completely eliminate duress as a reasonable possibility.
