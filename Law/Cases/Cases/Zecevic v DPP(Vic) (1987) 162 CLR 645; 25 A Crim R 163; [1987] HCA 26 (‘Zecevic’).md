## Material Facts

Zecevic got into a heated argument with his neighbor, who then stabbed Zecevic in the chest, threatened to "blow his head off," and went toward his car. Believing the neighbor was going to get a gun from the car, Zecevic ran to his own unit, grabbed a shotgun, went back outside, and shot the neighbor dead. The trial judge withdrew the defence of self-defence from the jury entirely.

## Issues

What is the correct, simplified legal test for the common law defence of self-defence in Australia?

## Rule/Law

The High Court simplified self-defence into a single, two-part test:

**Did the accused believe upon reasonable grounds that it was necessary in self-defence to do what they did?**

1. **Subjective:** Did the accused _honestly believe_ their actions were necessary?
2. **Objective:** Were there _reasonable grounds_ in the circumstances for that belief? _Note: The prosecution bears the ultimate burden of disproving self-defence beyond reasonable doubt. The old "excessive force" rule that automatically reduced murder to manslaughter was completely abolished._

## Application/Analysis

Because Zecevic had actually been stabbed and threatened with death, there was enough evidence to raise a plausible claim of self-defence. Even though Zecevic went inside to get a gun first, whether his belief was genuine and based on reasonable grounds was a question of fact that only the jury—not the judge—had the legal right to decide.

## Conclusion

The High Court allowed the appeal, set aside the murder conviction, and ordered a new trial.

### Judges Rules

- **Rule 1: The Core Self-Defence Test**  
    A defendant is entitled to a total acquittal if **they believed on reasonable grounds that it was necessary in self-defence to do what they did**.
- **Rule 2: The Two-Step Assessment**  
    The test is a mix of subjective and objective elements:
    1. The accused must have **honestly and genuinely believed** their actions were necessary (subjective).
    2. A reasonable person in the accused's exact circumstances must also be able to see **reasonable grounds** for that belief (objective).
- **Rule 3: No Automatic Manslaughter for Excessive Force**  
    If a defendant genuinely believed their actions were necessary, but the jury decides there were _no reasonable grounds_ for that belief, **the defence of self-defence fails completely**. There is no automatic "excessive self-defence" rule to reduce murder to manslaughter, though the jury can still return a manslaughter verdict on other grounds, such as a lack of intent to kill.
