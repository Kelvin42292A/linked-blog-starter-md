## Material Facts

McFarland, Holland, and McConnell assaulted the deceased, stabbed him, tied him up, and threw him into a river with a concrete block, causing him to drown. McFarland and Holland claimed they only participated because McConnell threatened to take their lives. Because they both directly inflicted the violence (stabbing and drowning), they were charged with murder as "principals in the first degree" (the actual killers).

## Issues

Is the defence of duress legally available to a person charged with murder if they are a principal in the first degree (the actual killer)?

## Rule/Law

No. **Duress is never available as a defence to murder for a principal in the first degree**. While the law may allow duress as a defence for a "principal in the second degree" (someone who merely aids or abets the crime), the balance of judicial authority strictly forbids the actual killer from using threats to their own life as an excuse for murder.

## Application/Analysis

Because McFarland and Holland directly participated in the physical acts that caused the victim's death (the beating, stabbing, and throwing him in the river), they were classified as principals in the first degree. Therefore, regardless of how severely McConnell threatened them, the defence of duress was completely unavailable to them in law.

## Conclusion

The NSW Court of Criminal Appeal upheld the trial judge's decision to withdraw the defence of duress from the jury and dismissed the appeals, confirming their murder convictions.

### Judges Rules

When raising the defence of duress to a charge of murder, the law draws a strict, objective boundary:

1. **The "Actual Killer" Exclusion (First Degree):** The defence of duress is **completely excluded** for a principal in the first degree. The law dictates that you cannot intentionally take an innocent person's life to save your own. If you commit the actual physical acts of killing, you cannot rely on duress.
2. **The Aider/Abettor Distinction (Second Degree):** Under the authority applied in this case, the law distinguishes between degrees of participation. The defence of duress _may_ still be available to a principal in the second degree—someone who acts as a getaway driver or lookout, but does not actually commit the fatal violence themselves.
3. **Strict Legal Limits over Subjective Fear:** No matter how genuinely terrified the accused was, or how extreme the threats were, public policy and the common law impose a strict limit on the defence. If the charge is first-degree murder, the judge must completely withhold the defence of duress from the jury.
