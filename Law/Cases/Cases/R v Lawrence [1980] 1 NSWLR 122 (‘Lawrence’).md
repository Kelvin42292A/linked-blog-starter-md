## Material Facts

De Graaff was convicted of participating in a massive conspiracy to import cannabis by ship. He claimed he only navigated the smuggling vessel because other conspirators threatened to kill him and "blow his kneecaps off" if he went to the police.

## Issues

What **type of harm** must be threatened for an accused person to successfully rely on the defence of duress?

## Rule/Law

To excuse a crime, the type of harm threatened must be **immediate death or serious personal violence** to the accused or their family. The threat must be so severe that an **average person of ordinary firmness of mind** would have yielded to it. Trivial threats (like threatening to slap a spouse) or blackmail (like threatening to expose embarrassing photos) are not legally sufficient to justify committing a crime.

## Application/Analysis

While De Graaff claimed he faced threats of extreme violence (death and being crippled), he had multiple safe opportunities to escape the threats and report the crime—such as visiting a police sergeant friend in his home—but he failed to do so. Because he did not use these reasonable opportunities to escape, the defence failed.

## Conclusion

The Court of Criminal Appeal dismissed his appeal, confirming that the trial judge correctly instructed the jury on the strict type of harm required and the objective limits of the duress defence.

### Judges Rules (**Type of harm threatened)**

1. **Death or Serious Violence is Required:** The threat must involve **death or serious physical violence** directed at the accused or a close relative.
2. **The "Ordinary Firmness" Standard:** The threatened harm must be so severe that it would overpower the will and resistance of an **average, reasonable person of ordinary firmness of mind**.
3. **Trivial Threats & Blackmail Excluded:** The law does not allow people to commit serious crimes for minor reasons. Threats of minor physical harm, property damage (in most cases), or blackmail (ruining someone's reputation) do not legally excuse a crime.

### Judges Rules (**Ordinary firmness)**

Based on _Lawrence_, when assessing if a threat legally excuses a crime under duress, the law applies the following strict rules:

1. **The Objective Baseline:** The law does not just look at how frightened the accused was. The threat must be so severe that an **average person of ordinary firmness of mind** would have surrendered to it.
2. **Matching Characteristics:** This hypothetical "average person" is legally assumed to be of the **same age and sex** as the accused, and facing the **exact same circumstances and risks**.
3. **The Duty of the Ordinary Person:** The law expects ordinary people to show a basic level of courage and resistance. If a person of ordinary firmness would have taken a reasonable opportunity to escape the threat (such as going to the police), the accused must also take it, or they lose the defence.
