## Material Facts

Glen Conlon was at his remote home when he discovered two men (Hulands and Neill) stealing his marijuana plants. The men followed him inside, fractured his head with a heavy plate, and repeatedly kicked him. Conlon escaped, grabbed a loaded rifle, shot Neill in the abdomen, and chased Hulands outside, shooting him dead. He then returned and beat Neill with the rifle and an axe, and stabbed him in the throat. Conlon had consumed a near-entire bottle of whisky and smoked a massive amount of marijuana prior to the incident.

## Issues

Can a defendant’s voluntary intoxication (by alcohol and drugs) be taken into account when deciding if they had "reasonable grounds" to believe their actions were necessary in self-defence?

## Rule/Law

Yes. The test for self-defence is whether the accused believed on reasonable grounds that their actions were necessary. This is a mixed subjective and objective test—it evaluates what the _accused_ (not a hypothetical sober person) might reasonably believe in their actual circumstances. Therefore, **voluntary intoxication is legally relevant** and must be considered regarding how it affected the accused's perception of the threat and the reasonableness of their response.

## Application/Analysis

- _For Hulands:_ The Crown could not disprove that Conlon's intoxicated, fear-driven perception in the dark made his defensive shooting seem reasonable to him.
- _For Neill:_ Conlon knew Neill was severely injured and "posed no threat". Beating and stabbing him to "put him out of his misery" could not be believed to be necessary in self-defence.

## Conclusion

Conlon was acquitted of Hulands' murder because of self-defence, but was convicted of manslaughter for Neill's death because self-defence failed, though his liability was reduced by diminished responsibility.

### Judges Rules (**The general defence)**

1. **Intoxication is Relevant to "Reasonableness":** When determining if a defendant had "reasonable grounds" for their belief that force was necessary, the court must assess the situation as the **intoxicated defendant actually perceived it**.
2. **No "Sober Person" Standard:** Unlike other objective legal tests (such as provocation), the court does _not_ measure the defendant's actions against a hypothetical reasonable sober person.
3. **Limits of the Excuse:** Intoxication can support a mistaken belief in a threat, but it cannot excuse violence once the defendant admits they knew the victim no longer posed any danger.
